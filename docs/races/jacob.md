## 1. Identity, and which individual to depict

### 1.1 The breed in one paragraph
The Jacob is a British breed: piebald (white with black patches) and usually **polycerate**, meaning it has more than two horns [WP-EN]. For centuries it was kept as an ornamental "park sheep" on English estates. Spotted sheep are documented in England by the 1600s [LC][WP-EN]. It was formerly called "piebald" or "Spanish" sheep, and the name "Jacob", from Genesis 30, was applied in the 20th century [WP-EN][OSU]. The UK Jacob Sheep Society was founded in 1969, with a flock book from 1972 [WP-EN]. UK breeders deliberately enlarged the breed "from the light boned parkland sheep to the sturdy commercial animal of today" [JSS-INTRO]. American Jacobs, imported in small numbers since the early 1900s, "have not undergone improved breeding and out crossing to satisfy the commercial marketplace". They are slender-boned with a primitive body shape [JSBA-HIST][JSBA-ABOUT]. Both sexes are always horned, and polled animals cannot be registered in either country [JSS-INTRO][JSBA-STD][CF].

### 1.2 British vs American Jacob

| | British Jacob (Jacob Sheep Society, UK) | American Jacob (JSBA, USA) |
|---|---|---|
| General look | "alert, active sheep being upstanding and deep-bodied" [JSS-STD]; "sturdy commercial animal" [JSS-UKUS] | "fine-boned, primitive, smaller stature" [JSS-UKUS]; somewhat goat-like in appearance [LC] |
| Ram weight | 70–90 kg [JSS-UKUS] (Wikipedia: 80–100 kg) | 120–180 lb = 54–82 kg; over 180 lb disqualifies [JSBA-STD] |
| Ewe weight | 55–70 kg [JSS-UKUS] (Wikipedia: 60–65 kg) | 80–120 lb = 36–54 kg; over 130 lb disqualifies [JSBA-STD] |
| Horn number | "two or four" [JSS-STD]; British Jacobs "most often have two horns" (Horak & Horak 2010, cited by [WP-EN]) | "Any number provided they are well-differentiated … and balanced" [JSBA-HORN]; "more often polycerate" [WP-EN] |
| Colour rule | "White with well-defined black patches"; no percentage [JSS-STD] | about 60 % white and 40 % black or lilac; outside 15–85 % coloured disqualifies [JSBA-STD] |
| Legs | "Preferably white with little or no black" [JSS-STD] | "white with or without colored patches"; one to three dark legs acceptable [JSBA-STD] |
| Tail | Usually docked, but long: ring placed "one third from the tip" [JSS-INTRO] | Natural tail "reaches almost to the hock" is described as the norm; many are docked [JSBA-STD][JSBA-GB] |

### 1.3 Why a four-horned ram
- **Horn mass.** Ram horns are far larger. Ewe horns are thinner, shorter and more delicate [WP-EN][OSU][JSC-ABOUT]. At desktop-pet sizes, ewe horns barely break the head silhouette and get lost among the ears and wool [photo].
- **The iconic silhouette.** "Four-horned rams have two vertical centre horns which may be 61 cm (2 ft) or more in length", plus "two smaller side horns, which grow down along the sides of the head" [WP-EN, citing Horak & Horak][JSC-ABOUT][SAW]. No other sheep looks like that.
- **The rules are clearest for rams.** A multi-horned ram must carry at least four strong, cleanly separated, balanced horns. A four-horned ewe may legally have only scurs (loose, stunted horns) in the lower position [JSBA-HORN].
- **Why the American type.** It is more often polycerate [WP-EN] and keeps the long, upright centre horns. It still meets the UK four-horn rule: "the top pair should grow upward from the top of the head and have no forward growing tendencies. There should preferably be space between the top and lower horns" [JSS-STD].
- **Age.** Horn direction only becomes readable after about 6 months and can still change [JSBA-HORN]. Full length and a complete lower spiral need maturity, so I suggest 3 to 5 years (my recommendation).
- **Trade-off.** A ram should look "thicker set and masculine" [JSS-STD] and shows a (short) scrotum. Ewe differences are in sections 2.4 and 6.10.

### 2.4 Body
- **UK wording:** "Back - straight, level from base of neck to setting on of tail, which should be broad. Well sprung ribs, body well let down, forming a good bottom line. Tail to be well set up on chine and well-developed thigh." [JSS-STD]
- **US wording (paraphrased):** long-framed, smooth-muscled, fine-boned, well-sprung ribs, straight back, and a rump that slopes toward the tail head [JSBA-STD]. The tail head sits slightly below the line of the back, a sign the animal was never improved for leg muscle [JSBA-GB]. The carcass is lean with little external fat [JSBA-HIST].
- **For the drawing:** a long, rectangular barrel. The topline is level from withers to hips, then slopes gently over the croup to a tail head set just below the topline. The ribs are well sprung, the brisket sits at about elbow level, and the belly line is nearly straight with a slight tuck at the flank. The thighs are moderate, not the bulging hindquarters of a meat breed. The shoulder is slightly angular.
- **Ram vs ewe:** the ram has a heavier neck and forehand, a broader head, and is "thicker set and masculine". The ewe has a "fine feminine appearance" [JSS-STD].
- **Genitals:** rams have a **short scrotum held close to the body** [JSBA-STD], wool-free [WP-EN]. It may carry a dark breed marking [JSC-DESC]. Long, pendulous scrota are a disqualifying fault [JSBA-STD]. Ewes have a small, wool-free udder held close to the body [WP-EN].

### 2.5 Neck
"Strong, of medium length well set on shoulder" [JSS-STD]; "medium length" [JSBA-STD]. It is thicker in rams. In profile the neck rises from the withers at about 35–50°, with a gently convex crest. The throat line runs from the angle of the jaw to the point of the chest [photo].

### 2.6 Head size
The head is slender and triangular [JSBA-STD]: small, roughly triangular, not coarse [JSC-DESC]. A large, square, non-Jacob-type head disqualifies [JSBA-STD]. Use H ≈ 0.31 W, about 23 cm on a 75 cm ram [photo].

### 2.7 Legs vs body
Legs are "Medium boned of medium length" [JSS-STD], "Fine boned and of medium length" [JSBA-STD]. In fleece the visible leg is about half the body depth; shorn, it is closer to equal (see 2.3). See section 7 for detail.

### 2.8 Tail: natural or docked?
- **Natural:** "reaches almost to the hock" and "Is wooly, not hairy". "Fat or short tailed" disqualifies [JSBA-STD]. The JSBA guidebook calls Jacobs "medium-tailed": the tail reaches to the hock and the animal can lift it. Most are docked, at the breeder's choice [JSBA-GB]. The German register calls the breed "langschwänzig" (long-tailed) [LFL].
- **UK practice:** "the Jacob breed is a hill sheep so tails should only be docked sufficiently to keep them clean in adult life - hold the lambs tail out straight and place the rubber ring one third from the tip" [JSS-INTRO]. So about two thirds of the length is kept. Wikipedia says owners usually leave several inches [WP-EN]. Zoo animals are sometimes docked very short [SFZOO].
- **Recommendation:** draw the **natural tail**, which also animates well. Make it a woolly cylinder hanging from the tail head, broad at the root (about 0.25 H wide in fleece), tapering slightly to a rounded woolly tip a hand's width above the hock. It is white unless a rump patch reaches it. The alternative is the UK-docked tail at about two thirds of that length.

## 3. Fleece

### 3.1 Wool type (facts)
- **Single coat:** a "medium fine" fleece with "no outer coat", unlike other primitive breeds, which are double-coated [JSBA-ABOUT][JSBA-HIST][WP-EN]. The German register describes few guard hairs ("wenig Grannenhaaren") [LFL].
- **Fineness:** Bradford count 44–56 (26.4–36.2 µm) is the US ideal [JSBA-STD]. UK grading: "Jacob 1" is 54–58s (25–27.5 µm) and "Jacob 2" is 46–52s (30–33 µm) [JSS-INTRO]. Wikipedia gives 32–34 µm [WP-EN].
- **Staple length:** 3–7 in (7.5–18 cm) [JSBA-STD]; 75–180 mm [JSS-INTRO]; 7–18 cm [LFL]; 4–6 in [LC]; 8–15 cm [WP-EN].
- **Fleece weight:** 3–6 lb [JSBA-STD]; 2–2.5 kg [WP-EN].
- **Handle and look:** "open character and soft springy handle", little grease [JSBA-STD]. It is open and parts easily to the skin [JSC-DESC], "lofty, light, and open" [SPINOFF], "von offener Beschaffenheit und seidig glänzend" (open and silky-lustrous) [LFL], demi-lustre [JSBA-GB].
- **Crimp and kemp:** crimp often shows at the shoulders but not the hindquarters [SAW]. Quality varies over the body, often "running out at the britch" (the rear of the thighs), which is coarser [JSC-DESC][SPINOFF]. Kemp (coarse, chalky fibres), if present, sits on the hindquarters and lower hind legs [JSBA-GB][SAW]. The UK wants "little or no kemp" [JSS-STD].
- **Lambs:** lambs of primitive lines are born with a coat of guard hair, shed at 3–6 months [WP-EN][JSC-ABOUT].

### 3.2 What the fleece looks like
- **Recommended medium fleece, 4–6 cm:** dense, plush and finely crinkled. Crimp shows as tight small waves, giving a slightly "popcorn" surface, most visible on the neck and shoulders. The surface is slightly lumpy where staples clump. Black and white areas are the **same length**, with no step at the boundary. The silhouette is smooth with a soft fuzzy edge and no hanging locks [photo].
- **Full fleece, 10–15 cm (late winter to spring, before shearing):** distinct wavy locks, open, with a parting line along the back. The outline is shaggy, with an uneven fringe along the belly and britch. Black areas show **brown sun-bleached tips**. White wool looks cream to oatmeal and dirtier toward the belly and legs [photo][SPINOFF].
- **Quilting (avoid):** in some animals the black and white wool differ in length or texture, so the boundary shows a step, like quilting. Excessive quilting after the first shearing is disqualifying in the US [JSBA-QUILT]. Draw the boundary flush.
- **Freckling and mottling:** small dark flecks in white wool are acceptable in small amounts [JSBA-STD]. "Mottled wool and skin undesirable" [JSS-STD].

### 3.3 Wool-free areas
- **Face:** "Clear of wool forward of horns" [JSS-STD][JSBA-STD]. The face carries short, smooth hair. Lambs under 6 months may have baby wool forward of the horns [JSBA-STD].
- **Poll:** a white tuft of wool between and just behind the upper horn bases [photo]. The Jacob Sheep Conservancy states that the poll is always white [JSC-DESC].
- **Legs:** "clear of wool below knee and hock" [JSS-STD]. Wool below the knees and hocks is disqualifying [JSBA-STD] and suggests crossbreeding [JSBA-GB].
- **Belly:** wool there is sparse and very short [JSBA-COL].
- **Scrotum:** wool-free [WP-EN].

## 4. Colour and spotting pattern

### 4.1 How much colour
- "The Jacob is a randomly spotted sheep, basically white with colored spots or patches … Preferably an approximate 60% white and 40% black or lilac spotting" [JSBA-STD][JSBA-COL]. Fewer than 15 % or more than 85 % coloured markings disqualify [JSBA-STD]. JSBA pedigree data show a **median of 40 % coloured**, with means of 37–39 % [ANJOLA].
- Head and leg colour are **not** counted, because they are hair, not wool [JSBA-COL].
- Disagreement: some sources say "roughly 50 %" [SAW][JSC-ABOUT]. The UK standard gives no figure [JSS-STD].
- Genetics, useful for intuition: the breed is "genetically a black sheep with a recessive spotting pattern" [JSBA-COL].
- Markings are present from birth and stay the same for life [JSS-WOOL]. Only bleaching and greying change the shade (see 4.4).

### 4.2 Where the colour goes, region by region
The **breed-specific markings** are on the muzzle, around the eyes, the ears, the nape (often reaching the shoulders), knees, hocks, pasterns, and the scrotum or udder [JSC-DESC]. "Head and neck generally black with a white blaze on the face extending down the chest" [JSS-STD]. There is "a dark cape over the dorsal part of the neck and shoulders" [WP-EN], and generally a white chest from under the brisket to the throatlatch [JSC-DESC]. On sheep with a lot of white, "the dark is on the front half of the body, often a heavy dark patch over the shoulders and only small spots elsewhere" [JSBA-COL].

| Region | Typical colour |
|---|---|
| Poll and top of forehead | white |
| Face | white blaze; black eye and cheek patches; black or dark muzzle (see 4.7) |
| Ears | black, preferred in the UK [JSS-STD]; white, coloured or spotted also acceptable in the US [JSBA-STD] |
| Top and sides of neck | black "cape" |
| Throat, front of neck, chest | white, continuing the blaze |
| Shoulders | the largest black patch, joined to the cape |
| Ribs and flanks | 1–3 random irregular patches, often roughly vertical or oblique blotches |
| Back | small random spots, or the upper ends of flank patches |
| Hips, rump, outer thigh | often one patch |
| Belly | usually white |
| Legs | white, with small black marks at knees, hocks and pasterns |
| Tail | usually white |
| Scrotum or udder | often a dark marking |

### 4.3 Recommended layout for the near side (ram facing right)
1. **Head:** as in section 4.7.
2. **Neck:** a black cape covers the top and sides from behind the ears back to the withers. Its lower edge runs diagonally from just behind the angle of the jaw down to the point of the shoulder. The underside of the jaw, the throat, the front of the neck and the chest are white.
3. **Shoulder:** the cape continues as the largest patch, over the shoulder blade and down to about the elbow. Its lower edge is ragged, with one or two white bays biting into it.
4. **Ribs and flank:** two irregular blotches. One sits just behind the shoulder, running from the topline obliquely down to mid-side, about 15–20 % of body length wide. A smaller one sits mid-flank. White separates them.
5. **Hip and thigh:** one rounded patch over the hip and upper outer thigh, ending well above the hock.
6. Two or three small satellite spots, from coin to palm size, on the back or flank.
7. **White:** belly, lower flank, back of the thigh (britch) and tail.
8. **Legs:** as in section 4.9.

Together these should cover about **40 % of the woolled surface** on this side. A real Jacob is **not** symmetrical: the far side has a different random layout. If the sprite is simply mirrored to face left, that is an acceptable simplification. Each animal is individually recognisable from a distance by its pattern [WP-EN][SAW].

### 4.4 Patch edges, brown tips and greying
- **Ideal:** "well-defined black patches" [JSS-STD]. Spots are distinct, with no "bleeding" of colour into the white, and the coat is never mottled [JSBA-GB]. Patches are clear and distinct, though mottling and freckling do occur [JSC-DESC].
- **Shape:** organic, irregular blotches with lobed or jagged outlines, like torn paper, and rounded bays. They are never geometric. Edges are crisp in short fleece and softened by overlapping locks in long fleece [photo].
- **Brown tips: yes.** The outer tips of black wool sun-bleach to a "lovely, mahogany brown", a heritable trait [JSBA-GB]. Black fleece "may appear brown from sun-bleached tips" but is "black closer to the skin" [SPINOFF]. Black areas may fade to dark brown at the tips [OSU]. Black wool may sun-bleach to browns [JSC-ABOUT].
- **Greying with age:** "some sheep fade from black to gray" [JSBA-COL], a separate process from sun-bleaching [JSBA-GB].
- **The face and legs keep their true colour.** "Fleece may show brown sun-bleached tips and some sheep fade from black to gray, but the facial and leg markings retain the true color of the sheep." [JSBA-COL]
- **How to paint it:**
  - *Medium fleece:* body patches are near-black with a cool sheen, and a faint warm-brown cast along the sunlit topline.
  - *Full fleece:* the outer tips are clearly chocolate to mahogany, especially on the back and neck, with black showing in the parts and shadows.
  - *Always:* the face and leg markings stay jet black.

### 4.6 The lilac variant (if wanted instead of black)
- **Genetics:** lilac is a recessive dilution (MLPH gene) [WP-EN]. Black is dominant over lilac at a single locus [ANJOLA].
- **Words used:** "lilac" (brownish-grey) [WP-EN]; a light greyish-brown or fawn [JSBA-GB]; "blue-gray to gray-brown" [SAW]; deep grey with a blue or purple cast [JSC-ABOUT]; grey-brown facial markings [SPINOFF].
- **Face and legs:** lilac lambs are born with grey or grey-brown markings, and the wool is grey to grey-brown from birth [JSBA-COL]. The face and leg markings are lilac too, which is how breeders tell lilac from faded black [JSBA-COL].
- **Visual:** a warm taupe or mocha with a faint violet-grey cast. See the lilac tokens in 4.5. The one lilac lamb photographed also had dark grey-brown nose and hooves [photo, single example].
- I recommend **black** for the main design: it is far more common and reads with more contrast.

### 4.7 The face pattern ("badger face")
- **UK standard:** "It is desirable that there be a clear white blaze with even cheeks. In adult sheep pink noses in conjunction with broad white face undesirable. A dark nose is preferred." [JSS-STD] At UK society sales: "All sheep must have a clear white blaze running from the top of the head to the muzzle." [JSS-INSP]
- **US standard:** a clear white blaze from poll to muzzle, large symmetrical eye patches incorporating the cheeks, and a straight profile. The nasal septum and mouth should be dark in adults "even though the muzzle patch may be absent". Acceptable: only one of the three facial markings, an eye patch slipped below the eye, or a pink nose. Disqualifying: no facial markings at all [JSBA-STD].
- **"Badger-faced"** means black cheeks and black muzzle, with a white blaze down the front [OSU][JSC-ABOUT][WP-EN]. Face patches may be slight, or large and run together, but they always leave a white poll and a white blaze down to the black muzzle [JSC-DESC].
- **Front view:**
  - A white poll fills the space between the upper horn bases.
  - The blaze narrows between the eyes to about a third of the face width, runs down the nasal bridge, and often widens a little toward the nose.
  - Black eye patches start at the horn and ear base, enclose the eyes, and cover the cheeks down to the jaw.
  - The muzzle is black: nose pad, nostrils, lips and chin [photo].
- **Side view, which is what you draw:**
  - The blaze shows edge-on as a **white band along the top profile** from the horn bases to the nose. It is about the upper third of the head's depth at eye level, and widest at the forehead.
  - Everything below it on the side of the face is **black**: the eye, cheek, jaw muscle area, side of the muzzle, black nose leather and lips.
  - A thin white edge under the chin and jaw, joining the white throat, is common.
  - The ear is black [photo].
- **Variant B (common on UK show rams and the JSBA's own example ram):** the blaze widens over the whole muzzle, so only the nose pad and nostrils are black. This is equally correct, but a little less "badger".
- **Faults:** a missing or broken blaze (rejected at UK sales); no facial markings; a pink nose with a broad white face (UK).
- **Do not confuse** this with the "Badger Face" Welsh Mountain sheep, which is a different breed and pattern.

### 4.8 Ear colour
"Black ears preferred" [JSS-STD, web version]. Oklahoma State says the nose, ears and horns should be black [OSU]. JSBA accepts white, coloured or spotted ears [JSBA-STD]. Draw **black** ears, dark grey inside with sparse hair.

### 4.9 Leg colour and spotting
- UK: "Preferably white with little or no black" [JSS-STD].
- US: "white with or without colored patches". One to three dark legs are acceptable; all dark legs disqualify [JSBA-STD]. Jacobs never have four black legs [JSBA-GB].
- Breed markings sit on the knees, hocks and pasterns, plus random spots [JSC-DESC]. Oklahoma State calls black knees and hocks desirable [OSU].
- **Draw:**
  - Bright white legs (hair).
  - An oval black spot on the front of each knee (carpus), about 0.2 H tall.
  - A black patch on the point or outer side of each hock.
  - Small black marks just above the hoof on one or two feet.

### 4.10 Hoof colour
"Hooves are black or striped". White hooves are acceptable but less desirable [JSBA-STD], and the JSBA guidebook says not white [JSBA-GB]. Hooves are usually black [LC][OSU]; the UK summary simply says "black hooves" [JSS-UKUS]. **Draw black hooves.** Below a white coronet (the band where the hoof meets the leg), a vertical pale stripe running from coronet to toe gives the "striped" hoof [photo].

### 4.11 Skin
"It is preferred than [sic] skin beneath white area to be a good pink, whilst black beneath the dark wool" [JSS-STD], and the US standard says the same [JSBA-STD]. Visible skin: the black nose leather, lips and eyelids inside the eye patches; ear skin; the scrotum or udder; and belly skin on a shorn animal.

## 5. Head

- **Shape:** slender and triangular from the front and from above [JSBA-STD], with a "narrow triangular" face and small ears [JSC-HIST]. The ram's head is broader and heavier than the ewe's [JSS-STD].
- **Profile:** straight is desirable. A slight Roman nose is acceptable but less desirable, and a large, square head disqualifies [JSBA-STD]. In side view:
  - The forehead is flat from the horn base to between the eyes.
  - The nasal line is straight, with at most a slight convexity just above the nostrils.
  - The muzzle tapers to a narrow, rounded nose.
  - The jaw is relatively shallow; its lower line is nearly straight and rises gently to the chin [photo].
- **Muzzle and nostrils:** the muzzle is narrow [SFZOO]. Nostrils are oblique, comma-shaped slits at the front of the nose, in black nose leather. The upper lip is cleft down the middle, a sheep trait [SFZOO].
- **Mouth and teeth:** small, with black lips. Sheep have no upper front teeth: the lower incisors meet a hard pad in the upper jaw [SFZOO][JSS-INTRO]. When animating chewing or bleating, show only lower incisors. The mouth must be "correct", neither overshot nor undershot [JSS-INSP].
- **Eyes:**
  - *Colour:* "Dark bold eyes preferred with no tendency to split eyelids deformity" [JSS-STD]. US acceptable colours are brown, blue or marbled [JSBA-STD]. The German register asks for "klaren, großen Augen" (clear, large eyes) [LFL]. In photos the iris is most often **amber to mid-brown**.
  - *Pupil:* **horizontal and rectangular**, like all grazing sheep.
  - *Rotation:* when the head drops to graze, the eyeballs rotate to keep the pupil parallel to the ground, by **50° or more** [BANKS].
  - *Placement:* high on the side of the head, about 0.4 H behind the poll, just below and in front of the horn roots, slightly bulging. The eyelids are black, inside the patch [photo]. The eye can vanish in the black patch, so give it a clear amber iris and a small highlight.
  - *Never draw* a notch or split in the upper eyelid. That defect is linked to the four-horn genes and is rejected or penalised by both societies [JSS-INSP][JSBA-STD][WP-EN].
- **Ears:**
  - *Wording:* "Small, erect and slightly above the horizontal" [JSBA-STD]; "klein, aufrecht und leicht oberhalb der Horizontalen" [LFL]. The Jacob Sheep Conservancy describes them as small to medium, held slightly above horizontal, and never large, pendulous or erect [JSC-DESC].
  - *Meaning:* carried out sideways and roughly horizontal with the tip slightly raised. Not pricked straight up like a goat's, not drooping. "Large pendulous ears" disqualify [JSBA-STD].
  - *Shape and placement:* leaf-shaped, about 0.3–0.35 H long, set below and behind the horn roots, level with or a little above the eye. On a four-horned ram the ear is partly hidden **inside the curl of the lower horn**, with its tip poking out backwards [photo].
- **Poll:** a white wool tuft between and just behind the upper horn bases [photo][JSC-DESC].

## 6. Horns

### 6.1 Basics
- **Both sexes are horned.** "Polled sheep are NOT accepted in the Flock Book" [JSS-INTRO], and polled sheep disqualify in the US [JSBA-STD].
- **Number:** two or four [JSS-STD]. In the US, any number if they are balanced and differentiated [JSBA-STD]. Two, four or six [LFL]. A desirable multi-horned ram has four, "or more rarely six", strong horns [JSBA-HORN].
- **Inheritance (sources disagree):**
  - JSBA: "two versus four – is definitely hereditary, with four horns being dominant" [JSBA-HORN].
  - A study of JSBA pedigree records found neither type completely dominant, with an effect depending on which parent carries four horns [ANJOLA].
- **Lambs:** ram lambs are born with horn buds already showing [OSU][JSC-ABOUT].

### 6.2 Where the horns grow from (the skull)
- **Upper pair** (the front, or "rostral", set): grows from the top of the skull at the poll, close to the midline. The left and right bases are separated by skin and the white poll tuft [photo, RVC skull][WP-EN].
- **Lower pair** (the rear-and-side, or "caudal", set): on each side, directly below and slightly behind the upper base. It sits above and behind the eye, in front of the ear [photo, RVC skull][WP-EN].
- **Gap between the pairs:**
  - "Flesh between horn bases" is desirable [JSBA-STD].
  - "There should preferably be space between the top and lower horns" [JSS-STD].
  - At UK sales, "Any four-horned sheep with forward horns or fused horns (not having wool/hair/skin between the top and side horns) will be rejected" [JSS-INSP].
  - Wikipedia: "On polycerate animals it is preferred that there is a fleshy gap between the two pairs of horns" [WP-EN].
  - The German register: "zwischen den Hornbasen soll Haut sein" (there should be skin between the horn bases) [LFL].
  - **Draw** a strip of hair or skin about half to one horn-base width between the upper and lower roots on the near side.

### 6.3 The upper pair
- **Sources:**
  - "two vertical centre horns which may be 61 cm (2 ft) or more" [WP-EN][JSC-ABOUT][SAW].
  - "The rostral set of horns usually extend upwards and outwards" [WP-EN].
  - "the top pair should grow upward from the top of the head and have no forward growing tendencies" [JSS-STD].
  - The Jacob Sheep Conservancy describes a ram's centre horns "growing straight up", about two feet long, tapering to very sharp points; breeders prize upright, symmetrical horns [JSC-NL].
  - The four horns should be "growing up or out in a manner that will not interfere with the animal's health" [JSBA-HORN].
- **Shape, from photos:**
  - The horns rise from the poll and diverge in a V, at about 40–70° between them when seen from the front.
  - Each is nearly straight to gently curved backward and outward, with a slight spiral twist.
  - The tip is slender and sharp.
- **In side view:** they lean back from vertical by about **15–30°** on the American type, or **35–55°** on UK show rams, whose horns are shorter, thicker and more strongly curved. The rear edge is concave. They **never** tip forward over the face [photo].

### 6.4 The lower pair
- **Sources:**
  - "two smaller side horns, which grow down along the sides of the head" [WP-EN].
  - "the caudal set of horns curls downwards along the side of the head and neck" [WP-EN].
  - In four-horned animals the lower horns "grow in a spiral shape" [WP-EN].
  - They must be "well-spaced from face, cheeks and neck" [JSBA-STD].
  - The lower pair must leave room between horn and jaw [LFL].
  - The lower pair should not have a double curl [JSBA-GB].
- **Shape, from photos:**
  - The horn leaves the skull sideways and backward and sweeps back over the ear.
  - It turns down behind the angle of the jaw, then curves forward along the lower cheek and upper throat.
  - The tip points forward, and slightly out or up, at about jaw or throat level. The overall shape is a C, or three-quarters of a ring, around the ear.
  - It is clearly thinner and shorter than the upper horn.

### 6.5 How to draw the four horns in profile (ram facing right)
1. **Upper root:** mark the poll, at the top rear of the head. The near upper horn's base is an oval about 0.3 H long.
2. **Near upper horn:** from the poll, sweep up and slightly back, about **1.6–1.8 H** long for a striking but realistic American ram. Curve it gently and taper it to a sharp point.
3. **Far upper horn:** its root is hidden behind the near root. Its shaft appears just behind and above the near horn, with the tip about 0.25 H further back, and it looks slightly shorter because it diverges outward. **Both tips must read as two separate points.** This is what makes the four-horned silhouette read.
4. **Near lower root:** just below and behind the upper root, about 0.15–0.25 H lower, with the hair strip from 6.2 between them.
5. **Lower horn path:** back, then down behind the ear base, then forward along the lower cheek and throat. The loop is about **0.8–1.0 H tall and 0.6 H wide**. The tip ends **clear** of the jaw and cheek by about 0.1–0.2 H (a finger to a fist in real life), pointing forward.
6. **Ear:** emerges below the horn roots, behind the eye. Its tip shows through or below the lower horn loop, pointing back.
7. **Far lower horn:** mostly hidden by the head. Optionally, show a sliver of its lowest arc or tip below the throat line.
8. **Stacking order, back to front:** far upper horn, far lower horn, neck and head, near upper horn, near ear, near lower horn.

### 6.6 Dimensions relative to the head (H about 23 cm) [photo, approximate]

| Part | Base diameter | Length | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper horn, mature ram | 0.25–0.33 H | 1.5–2.5 H (US; up to 61 cm or more); 1.3–1.5 H (UK show rams) | tapers to a sharp point |
| Lower horn, mature ram | 0.18–0.22 H | arc about 1.5–2 H; loop 0.6–1.1 H tall | clearance from face, cheek and neck |
| Gap between upper and lower roots | about 0.1–0.2 H | | skin or wool must show |
| Ewe horns | about 0.12–0.15 H | 0.5–1.2 H | fine and delicate |

### 6.7 Cross-section, surface and colour
- **Cross-section [photo]:** the upper horns are rounded-triangular to flattened-oval at the base, with a flatter front face, becoming round toward the tip. The lower horns are oval to round.
- **Surface [photo]:** dense transverse ridges ring each horn, perpendicular to its axis. They are deepest at the base and on the outer and front faces. A few deeper grooves mark annual growth. The tip is smooth and slightly paler from wear. At illustration scale, about 15–25 ridges read on a 1.6 H upper horn.
- **Colour:**
  - "Black horns are preferred" [JSS-STD].
  - "black or black and white striped" is desirable, and "solid white horns" disqualify [JSBA-STD][JSBA-HORN].
  - Horns are normally black, sometimes striped; white is undesirable [WP-EN].
  - *How they look:* weathered black horn is a very dark grey-brown with paler ridge crests. Show-prepared horn is glossy jet black with cool highlights. Striped horn has an ivory-tan ground with dark streaks running lengthwise along the twist.
  - **Use black** for the main design.

### 6.8 Acceptable variation (do not "correct" these into faults)
- **UK show rams:** shorter, thicker upper horns leaning back strongly, and large circular lower horns [photo, references 1 and 2].
- **Wide-sweeping upper horns:** going out sideways and down, like handlebars or a big ram curl. These are common in zoo and park animals [photo, references 4, 8 and 13]. They are acceptable unless they curl forward, but lose the "vertical centre horns" look.
- **Six horns:** acceptable in the US if all are distinct. The UK standard says two or four.
- **Fused horns (sources disagree):** "Fused, even horns" are acceptable in US rams [JSBA-STD], but fused horns are **rejected** at UK sales [JSS-INSP]. An evenly fused four-horned set can look two-horned [JSBA-HORN].
- **Minor contact:** in the US, upper and lower horns that touch at the base or show minor fusing are acceptable, as are slightly forward-tipping horns that do not impair grazing [JSBA-HORN][JSBA-STD].

### 6.9 Faults to avoid drawing (rejected or disqualified by the standards)
- **Fused horns:**
  - *UK:* fused horns with no wool, hair or skin between the top and side horns [JSS-INSP].
  - *US:* horns fused in an irregular pattern on one or both sides ("undifferentiated"). Fused horns often show a seam and split tips [JSBA-HORN].
- **Forward-pointing horns:**
  - *UK:* any forward horns [JSS-INSP][JSS-STD].
  - *US:* "Forward-pointing horns curling toward the eyes, or growing over the nose impairing the ability to graze naturally" [JSBA-STD].
- **Lower horns growing into the face:** "Lower, lateral horns that grow into the face, cheek, or neck" [JSBA-STD]. A narrow lower set may "curl in towards the face, neck or jaw" [JSBA-HORN].
- **Rear-curling horns** that would hit the neck or shoulders [JSBA-HORN].
- **Narrow two-horned set:** horns close to the jaw or neck on two-horned rams [JSBA-STD]. At UK sales an inspector must be able to fit fingers between face and horn [JSS-INSP].
- **Unbalanced or weak rams:** three or five horns, or fewer than four strong horns, or scurs instead of horns, on a multi-horned ram [JSBA-STD][JSBA-GB].
- **Colour:** solid white horns [JSBA-STD].
- **Polled:** no horns at all [JSS-INTRO][JSBA-STD].
- **General rule:** horns "should at all times give the animal freedom from injury and comfort when feeding" [JSS-STD].

### 6.10 Ewes
- "Horns firm, fine featured with femininity, balanced, and well differentiated" [JSBA-STD]. They are thinner, shorter and more delicate than a ram's [WP-EN][OSU].
- **Acceptable on ewes:** an unbalanced set, fused horns, or scurs in place of the lower horns. Upper horns must be sound [JSBA-HORN].
- **Four-horned ewe** (references 9 and 14 show this kind of fine horn set; their sex is not stated): two short upright spikes, about 0.6–1.2 H, leaning slightly back. The side horns are small, curving back and down near the ears, or are just scurs.
- **Two-horned ewe [photo, reference 3]:** one pair curving back and out, with tips turning out or forward in a short C, about 0.6–0.8 H.
- **Body:** smaller (65–70 cm at the withers), with a finer head and neck, and a small udder close to the body [LFL][JSS-STD][JSBA-STD].

### 6.11 Two-horned animals
- **Ram:**
  - *Shape:* "Two-horned rams typically have horizontal double-curled horns" [WP-EN]; the "classic horizontal double curled horn" [JSC-ABOUT][SAW]. The JSBA guidebook wants large horns that grow clear of the face in a wide-sweeping curl, like Bighorn or Karakul horns, not a tight, knobby curl [JSBA-GB].
  - *Clearance:* "wide, well-spaced horns with good clearance of cheeks, nose, and mouth at maturity" [JSBA-STD]. In the UK, space between the roots at the crown and between horn and cheek [JSS-STD], and room for the inspectors' fingers [JSS-INSP].
  - *Side view:* the horn rises from the top and side of the head, sweeps back, down around the ear, and forward. It forms a broad spiral that may start a second turn (the double curl). The tip points forward and out beside the jaw without touching it.
- **Ewe:** see 6.10.
- **Note:** an evenly fused four-horned animal can look two-horned [JSBA-HORN].

### 6.12 Horns and age
Ram lambs are born with buds [OSU]. Direction can rarely be judged before 6 months, and even after that "change can occur" [JSBA-HORN]. Horns keep growing with age; the spiral of the lower pair and the full length of the upper pair belong to mature rams. Breakage is common in ewe lambs [JSBA-HORN].

## 7. Legs and hooves

- **UK:** "Medium boned of medium length, clear of wool below knee and hock. Preferably white with little or no black. All sheep shall be correct on their pasterns." [JSS-STD] Weak or slack pasterns fail UK inspection [JSS-INSP].
- **US:** set square on all corners, fine-boned and of medium length, free of wool below the knees. Slight cow hocks (hocks turned in) are acceptable [JSBA-STD]. Cow hocks are common in the breed [WP-EN]. Legs should be well placed, without bow-legs, cow hocks or knock-knees, and the body should not be narrow [JSBA-GB].
- **Drawing notes [photo]:**
  - *Bones and joints:* slender cannon bones with clearly marked knee and hock joints.
  - *Fore legs:* vertical under the shoulder.
  - *Hind legs:* the cannon is vertical below the hock.
  - *Pasterns:* short and firm, angled about 50–60° (estimate).
  - *Feet:* small, neat, cloven (two toes).
  - *Hair:* the legs carry short white hair, not wool, from just above the knee and hock down.
- **Colours:** see 4.9 and 4.10. White legs with small black knee and hock spots; black hooves, which may be striped below white coronets.

## 8. Temperament, behaviour, vocalisation and gait (for animation)

### 8.1 Character
- "alert, active" [JSS-STD]. Docile, calm, social, intelligent, and good climbers [LC]. Goat-like curiosity and agility [OSU]. They "act more goat-like than other sheep" and browse brush [HOBBY].
- **Loose flocking:**
  - They have a loose flocking instinct, "preferring to be led rather than herded" [JSC-DESC].
  - "Jacobs do not show much flocking behaviour" [WP-EN].
  - "They can be skittish if not used to people" [WP-EN], but daily handling makes them tame [WP-EN].
  - Owners describe them as curious, with distinct personalities, and often more interactive with people than other breeds [JSS-WOOL].
- **Head carriage:** "up-headed" [JSC-DESC]. Even when relaxed, the head is carried proudly.
- **Agility:** Jacobs can jump or squeeze through weak fencing; fences of at least 1.2 m are advised [VETV] (a care guide, lower reliability).
- **Rams:** usually protective but not aggressive toward the flock [JSC-DESC]. Individual rams can be dangerous: one breeder describes a ram's two-foot horns tapering to very sharp points [JSC-NL].
- **Ewes:** protective mothers that sometimes use their horns against predators [HOBBY]. They lamb easily, and lambs are up and nursing quickly [JSBA-ABOUT].

### 8.2 Postures and actions
- **Alert:**
  - Head high, ears swung forward, a fixed stare.
  - **Stamping a forefoot** is an alarm signal [MERCK].
  - A threatened flock packs tightly, then may go still and silent [MERCK]. When disturbed, Jacobs cluster and run as a unit led by an older animal [SFZOO].
- **Grazing:**
  - Head down to the ground, fore legs straight, often one forefoot a little ahead.
  - The **pupils stay horizontal** because the eyeballs rotate [BANKS].
  - Sheep graze in bouts of 20–90 minutes, alternating with rest [MERCK].
  - The horn tips must clear the ground and muzzle. The standards require "comfort when feeding" [JSS-STD] and penalise horns that impair grazing [JSBA-STD].
  - Wide horns can make drinking from a half-full bucket awkward [JSS-INTRO]: a nice gag.
- **Browsing:** reaching up and forward into shrubs, goat-like [HOBBY].
- **Resting:** lying on the chest with the legs folded under, chewing the cud with a sideways grinding jaw (general sheep behaviour). Active by day, resting at night [MERCK].
- **Rams:**
  - Head-butting between rams [MERCK]. A typical sheep clash: back up, lower the head, charge, and meet forehead to forehead (general sheep behaviour). On a Jacob the upper horn bases take the impact.
  - Rams vocalise during courtship [MERCK]. They also curl the upper lip to test scent (flehmen), stretch the neck low, and kick a foreleg sideways at the ewe (general sheep behaviour).
- **Tail:**
  - A natural tail can be lifted by the animal [JSBA-GB].
  - Flicks at flies (general).
  - A ewe about to lamb holds her tail away from the body [JSS-INTRO].
  - Lambs wiggle their tails while suckling (general).

### 8.3 Vocalisation
- Mature Jacobs are not noisy. Lambs bleat, and ewes recognise their own by sound, sight and smell [SFZOO].
- Sheep rarely call except in three cases [MERCK]:
  - ewe and lamb exchange low-pitched bleats when close;
  - rams vocalise when courting;
  - an isolated or stressed animal gives a high-pitched bleat.
- **Animation suggestion (general sheep):**
  - *Bleat:* head lifts a little, the jaw drops moderately, the tongue shows, and the flank pulses.
  - *Soft ewe–lamb call:* mouth almost closed, nostrils flaring, short low "mmh".
  - *Ram's courting call:* a low rumble or grunt.

### 8.4 Gait (general sheep locomotion; nothing breed-specific was found)
- **Walk:** four beats in lateral sequence (left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore), with a slight head nod at each forefoot strike.
- **Trot:** diagonal pairs, head steady. This is the usual travelling gait.
- **Canter or gallop:** when fleeing, often with the flock bunched.
- **Jumps:** Jacobs are notably agile, light-footed and goat-like [OSU][HOBBY][LC]. Lambs play by bouncing straight up on stiff legs, often twisting sideways in the air.

## 9. The 10 details that make it instantly a Jacob

1. **Four horns.** An upright pair from the top of the head, and a lower pair spiralling down beside the face. A visible gap separates the pairs, and the lower pair stays clear of the face.
2. **Piebald fleece:** white with large, irregular, crisp-edged black patches, about 40 % coloured, concentrated on the neck and shoulders.
3. **Badger face:** black eye-and-cheek patches and a dark muzzle, split by a clean white blaze from poll to nose.
4. **Black head and neck "cape"**, with a white throat and chest continuing the blaze.
5. **White poll tuft** between the horns. The face is wool-free forward of the horns.
6. **Black horns with heavy ring-ridges**, or black-and-white striped. Never solid white, never curling forward.
7. **Slender white legs, wool-free below knee and hock,** with small black spots at the knees, hocks and pasterns, and **black hooves**.
8. **A lean, long, goat-like frame:** fine bones, straight back, head carried high. Not a round, woolly meat sheep.
9. **Small, near-horizontal black ears** tucked under the horn roots and inside the lower horn's curl.
10. **Black patches with sun-bleached chocolate tips** on the body, while the face and leg markings stay jet black. The eye is amber with a horizontal pupil, set in the black patch.
