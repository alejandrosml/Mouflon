# Ouessant sheep (mouton d'Ouessant, Ushant sheep): visual specification for a side-view illustration

Subject: **one mature black Ouessant ram, standing in full profile, facing right** (the viewer sees the animal's LEFT side).
Breed in one line: the smallest naturally occurring sheep breed, from the windswept island of Ouessant (Ushant) off Brittany. It is a perfectly proportioned miniature sheep, **not a dwarf**. Its coat is a single uniform colour (classically black). The ram carries one pair of strong horns, each forming a single wide spiral around the ear. The ewe is hornless. The tail is short and thin.

How to read the tags used below:
- **[STD]** comes from a breed standard: the French GEMO standard, the UK Ouessant Sheep Society (OSS) standard, or the Italian ASSONAPA breed sheet.
- **[SRC]** comes from another written source.
- **[PHOTO]** was observed by me in the reference photos that the GEMO standard page and the OSS guide use to illustrate the standard (see section 10.2).
- **[EST]** is my own estimate, measured off those photos and drawings. No published figure exists for it.
- Source numbers such as S1 point to section 10.

## 1. Identity: which individual to depict, and why

### 1.1 Choice: the ram
Depict a **mature black ram, about 3 to 5 years old**. Reasons:

1. **The horns are the breed's signature.**
   - The breed descriptions lead with them: "Cornes en spirale pour les béliers rappelant celles des mouflons" (S8).
   - The standards describe the ram's horns in detail and list specific horn disqualifications (S1, S4).
   - The OSS "Points of a Ouessant sheep" diagram is a ram (S4 p.2).
   - So is the GEMO standard's own example photo, captioned "Belle cravate et excellent cornage" ("fine cravat and excellent horns") (S1).
2. **The silhouette is unambiguous.** A tiny body carrying big, wide, curled horns reads at once as a miniature wild-type sheep. Farmers Weekly says the rams "remind me of a miniature version of the Bighorn sheep" (S13).
3. **A hornless ewe risks reading as a lamb.** A small black hornless sheep looks like a lamb or a generic black sheep. The Horniman Museum reports that visitors routinely mistake its fully grown 3-year-old Ouessant ewe for a lamb (S15).
4. **The ram shows extra breed marks**: the throat "cravate" (mane) and the very slight Roman nose (S1, S4).

### 1.2 The exact individual
- Black. Face and legs jet black. The body fleece is black with moderately sun-browned tips along the topline (see section 3). This is the colour the French standard calls "noire brunissante" ("browning black") (S1).
- Height about 46 to 48 cm at the withers. The maximum allowed is 49 cm; the OSS optimum for adult rams is 42 to 48 cm (S4).
- Weight about 16 to 18 kg.
- Horns of the "wide spiral" type: about one full turn, dark grey-brown, clear of the face (see section 6).
- Full autumn or winter fleece, with locks of about 6 to 8 cm. Full growth is 8 to 10 cm after 12 months.
- Pose: standing square and alert, head up, ears out sideways, tail down.

### 1.3 Differences for the ewe (if a ewe is also needed)

| Trait | Ram | Ewe |
|---|---|---|
| Horns | Strong, single wide spiral [STD] | **Polled (hornless)** [STD]. Rarely, small horns without a bony core ("petites cornes sans pivot osseux") or small, non-bony horn buds; not a fault (S1, S4). Draw her without. |
| Max height | 49 cm (optimum 42–48) | 46 cm (optimum 40–45) [STD] S1, S4 |
| Weight | about 13–20 kg | about 11–16 kg (see 2.2) |
| Profile | Very slight Roman nose, never excessive [STD] | Straight or very slightly convex. A Roman nose disqualifies a ewe [STD] S1, S4 |
| Throat | "Cravate": a mane of longer, coarser, often darker hair on the throat and nape [STD] | None or minimal |
| Neck and head | Thicker neck; broad poll carrying the horn bases | Finer head and slimmer neck; the wool topknot and the ears are fully visible |
| Fleece weight | 4.5 % of live weight [STD] | 4 % of live weight [STD] S1 |

A ewe must still read as adult. Give her a longer muzzle ("the muzzle lengthens in adulthood", S4), small ears relative to the head, full-length legs and a full fleece.

## 2. Size and proportions

### 2.1 Height at the withers (measured at the bony ridge between the shoulders, ideally just after shearing)

| Source | Ram | Ewe |
|---|---|---|
| GEMO standard, France, 1981/1991 [STD] S1 | **max 49 cm** | **max 46 cm** |
| OSS standard, UK [STD] S4 | max 49 cm; optimum adult 42–48, yearling 40–46 | max 46 cm; optimum adult 40–45, yearling 38–44 |
| ASSONAPA, Italy [STD] S9 | max 49 cm | max 46 cm |
| Wikipedia FR S6 | 42–49 cm | 40–46 cm |
| Wikipedia EN S7 / Farmers Weekly S13 | about 49 cm / about 19 in (48 cm) | about 45 cm / about 17 in (43 cm) |

- Final height is reached at about 3 years (S4).
- The limits are absolute: animals over the limit are deregistered (S4).
- The OSS also warns against breeding excessively small animals (S4).
- **Scale reference:** an adult stands roughly knee to mid-thigh high on an adult human.
- **Comparison:** the OSS gives Soay at 51–61 cm for rams and 49–54 cm for ewes (S4).

Lambs weigh about 1 kg at birth (S9; "around 2 lb", S13).

### 2.3 Body shape [STD]
- "Garrot non saillant, poitrine profonde": the withers are not prominent and the chest is deep (S1).
- "Ligne de dessus droite, du garrot à la base de la queue": the topline is straight from the withers to the root of the tail (S1). It must be level: not dipped (saddle-backed), not humped, and not higher at the rump. A high rump is a common fault (S4).
- "Bassin large": the pelvis and rump are broad and level (S1, S4).
- "Viewed from the side a Ouessant should be rectangular, with top and bottom lines parallel to the ground" (S4).
- **Not a dwarf.** GEMO is explicit: "ses membres ne sont pas atrophiés et son corps n'est pas raccourci … sa petite stature naturelle s'allie à une morphologie parfaitement proportionnée". The legs are not stunted and the body is not shortened (S2).
- Draw an ordinary, elegant sheep scaled down. Do not draw a chibi body with stubby legs.
- Overdeveloped limbs ("membres trop développés") are a disqualification (S1).

### 2.5 Neck
- [STD] "Rond, sans fanon, cravate chez les béliers": round, with no dewlap, and a cravat in rams (S1).
- [STD] OSS: "Full and rounded, without dewlap. Toggles or wattles are permitted but not desirable" (S4). In France, wattles under the throat ("pendeloques") are a disqualification (S1). **Draw no wattles.**
- [EST] The neck is short to medium and looks thick under the fleece. It joins the shoulders high and is carried at about 40–50° above horizontal when the animal is alert, so the poll sits clearly above the topline.
- **Ram:** a shaggy bib of long, coarse, wavy hair (the cravat) hangs from under the jaw down to the brisket, roughly to elbow level, and is often darker and glossier than the body fleece [PHOTO, STD]. Similar coarse mane hair grows on the nape (S4).

### 2.6 Head size
- [STD] "Fine et régulière" (S1). "Well formed and in proportion to the body; it should not be too broad" (S4).
- [EST] The head is about 0.4 H long, a neat wedge. Do not enlarge it the way lambs or cartoon sheep are drawn.

### 2.7 Tail
- [STD] "Short, above the hocks" (S4). "Coda corta sopra i garretti" (S9).
- A tail too long ("queue trop longue") disqualifies (S1). So does one hanging below the hocks (S9).
- It must be **thin**, not fluke-shaped or triangular like Nordic short-tailed breeds (S4).
- French studies cited by the OSS put it at **less than half the withers height** (S4).
- It is **natural and never docked**; docked animals cannot be registered in the UK (S4, S5).
- It is covered with wool. The amount of wool does not matter much (S4).
- [EST and PHOTO] In profile it hangs straight down, close against the buttocks, from the end of the level topline. It is partly lost in the breech wool and ends about one third to one half of the way from the tail root to the hock. It is the same colour as the fleece.
- Compared with its mainland relative, the Landes de Bretagne, it has a "queue plus courte" (shorter tail) (S6).

## 3. Fleece

### 3.1 Type and texture
- [STD] "Toison semi-fermée de bon tassé, mèches de 8 à 10 cm pour une pousse de 12 mois": a semi-closed, well-packed fleece, with staples of 8 to 10 cm after 12 months' growth (S1, S4, S9).
- [SRC] "Thick fleece of long wool with a dense undercoat" (S4, S5, S12). "Long-wool dual coated … very dense fine inner coat and longer outer coat" (S14).
- [STD] Hair and kemp (fibres with a medullary canal) are concentrated on the throat (the cravat, "surtout accentuée chez le bélier"), the nape and the lower thighs. They are the same shade as the fleece but may be darker (S1).
- [STD] Fibre diameter is 27–28 µm (S1). The OSS gives 25–28 µm (S4). The wool is "très douce au toucher, très souple": very soft and supple (S1).
- [PHOTO] **What this looks like:**
  - The surface is broken into distinct wavy-to-curly locks with slightly twisted, pointed tips. It is not a smooth, closed carpet like a Merino's.
  - On the neck, shoulders and flanks of a long-fleeced animal, the locks separate and hang in short ropes.
  - The outline along the back is softly lumpy.
  - The locks hang longest along the underline: throat, brisket and belly.
- The fleece is **shorn once a year in spring** (S10). It does not moult on its own like the Soay's ("Ouessant need to be shorn", owner report, S26).
- Just after shearing the animal looks sleek, angular, leggier and blacker (S4).

### 3.2 Extent: where the wool is and where it isn't
- [STD] "Toison garnissant le front, une partie des joues, revêtant tout le corps et descendant au moins jusqu'aux genoux et jusqu'aux jarrets" (S1). The fleece covers the forehead, part of the cheeks and the whole body, and comes down at least to the knees and hocks (S1, S4, S9).
- **Head:** a woolly **topknot cap** covers the forehead between and in front of the ears (and the horn bases in rams), roughly down to eye level. The wool runs onto the cheek behind the eye.
- **The face from the eyes to the nose is wool-free**, with short, fine, sleek black hair [PHOTO]. The ears are also short-haired.
- **Legs:** woolly "trousers" reach the knees and the hocks. Below them the legs have short sleek black hair, with no wool socks [STD and PHOTO].

### 3.3 How the black fleece looks: sun-browning
- [STD] The French standard lists "noire brunissante" (browning black) as an accepted colour, next to black, brown and white (S1).
- [STD] OSS: "Black fleeces often develop a brown cast, while the shorter hair on the face and legs remains black." Some blacks are "less susceptible … described as having a 'strong black' fleece". The base colour shows again after shearing (S4).
- [STD] ASSONAPA: "Per i velli neri, i peli possono scolorirsi sotto l'azione del sole (il vello diventa più o meno marrone)": in black fleeces the fibres can fade in the sun and the fleece turns more or less brown (S9).
- [PHOTO] **Yes, the tips sun-bleach to a rusty chocolate brown.** Each lock is black at the base and warm rust-brown at the tip. The effect is strongest on the most sun-exposed surfaces: back, top of the neck, upper flanks and rump. The belly, lower flanks, inner parts of the locks and the ram's cravat stay near black.
  - Example: GEMO's 1-year-old ram "Onyx" has a deep rust-brown outer fleece, but its face and legs are black.
  - Example: the OSS black ram in side view is a "strong black" with only a faint brown cast.
- **Recommendation:** a near-black body with a restrained warm-brown glint on the lock tips along the topline and upper flanks. Face and legs jet black. Too much brown makes it read as a brown (moorit) sheep.
- [STD] Other accepted modifiers, which are better avoided for a "classic black" portrait (S4):
  - age greying: white fibres giving a salt-and-pepper look, possible from 18–24 months;
  - genetic dilution to grey.
- Older animals may also grey around the face (seen in GEMO's 8-year-old ewe "Glycine") [PHOTO].

## 5. Head (profile, facing right)

- **Shape:** "Fine et régulière" [STD] S1. In profile it is a neat, fairly long wedge tapering to a fine muzzle, not broad and not blocky (S4). The muzzle lengthens with age (S4), so an adult has a longer face than a lamb.
- **Profile line:**
  - [STD] "Chanfrein et front formant une ligne continue. Très léger chanfrein chez le bélier." The forehead and nasal bridge form one continuous line, very slightly arched in the ram (S1).
  - OSS: "A slight bridge to the nose in the ram, flat or very slightly bridged in the ewe, not concave" (S4).
  - Disqualified: a Roman nose in the ewe, or too much of one in the ram (S1). **Never dished (concave).**
  - For the ram: one smooth, very gently convex line from the poll (hidden under the topknot and horn bases) to the nose tip.
- **Eye:**
  - [STD] "Brillant, regard vif, orbites saillantes": bright, lively look, with prominent orbits (S1). It should stand out from the face without looking pop-eyed (S4).
  - [EST and PHOTO] Placement: set laterally, high on the side of the head, about one third of the head length back from the poll, at the junction of the woolly forehead and the bare face.
  - The bony rim of the orbit makes a slight bulge in the profile.
  - The eye is an almond shape with a heavy black upper lid and lashes. The upper lid often covers the top of the iris. Little or no white shows.
- **Pupil:**
  - It is a horizontally elongated rectangle, as in all grazing prey species. It gives panoramic vision for spotting ground predators (S20).
  - Grazing animals' eyes "can rotate by 50 degrees or more" to keep the pupil horizontal when the head goes down to graze (S20). **For animation:** keep the pupil level with the ground when the head is lowered.
- **Iris:** pale honey-gold, amber or pale silvery. See section 4.
- **Nose and nostrils:**
  - [PHOTO, general sheep anatomy] A small, bare, black nose at the front of the muzzle. The nostrils are narrow oblique slits shaped like commas, curving up and back.
  - The upper lip is split by a central groove (philtrum), as in all sheep. The lips are thin and black, with a small chin.
  - [STD] The jaw should be neither undershot nor overshot. An undershot jaw shows the front teeth in a "permanent smile" and is a fault (S4). Draw the mouth closed and neat.
- **Ears:**
  - [STD] "Fine, petite, courte, mobile, tendant à se dresser": fine, small, short, mobile, tending to prick (S1).
  - OSS: "Fine, small, short, mobile, rounded at the tip and have a tendency to be carried pricked". "Carried pricked" means **close to horizontal, pointing outwards**, neither upright nor drooping. Not sharply pointed (S4).
  - Large ears disqualify (S1). So do large ears that are not held up (S9).
  - [EST] About one third of head length, leaf-shaped with a rounded tip, dark inside.
  - Placement: just behind the eye at about eye level.
  - **Ram:** the ear comes out just below and behind the horn base and sits inside the curl of the horn. The horn passes behind it, wraps under it and comes forward, so the ear is framed by the spiral. Wikipedia FR puts it as "cornes torsadées qui s'enroulent autour des oreilles", twisted horns that wind around the ears (S6). Seen from the side, the ear often points backward and outward through the curl and is partly hidden by the horn [PHOTO].
  - **Ewe:** the ear is fully visible, held horizontally or slightly raised, and points outward and backward.
  - The ears are **mobile**: animate them swivelling and flicking independently.
- **Topknot:** a cap of curly wool over the forehead, roughly down to eye level and onto the cheek behind the eye. In the ram it fills the space between and in front of the horn bases. Below the eyes the face is bare short hair (see 3.2).

## 6. Horns of the ram

### 6.1 What the standards say
- [STD, France, S1] "Sombre pour les noirs et les bruns, claire pour les blancs, section triangulaire, forte, enroulée d'une seule spirale de grande amplitude à bonne distance de la tête." That is: dark for black and brown sheep, pale for white ones, triangular in cross-section, strong, curled in a single spiral of large amplitude at a good distance from the head.
- [STD, France, S1] Disqualifications: horns "trop dissymétriques, ou trop frêles, ou trop serrées, ou trop écartées. Absence de cornes." That is: too asymmetric, too frail, too tightly coiled or too widely splayed, or no horns at all.
- [STD, UK, S4] Same wording in English. In addition:
  - Horns that are too close to the head or touching it are a fault.
  - "Excessively straight horns, that do not display a clear spiral are not permitted."
  - The **welfare test**: "A flat hand held vertically is okay; the same hand fitting horizontally between the head and the horn is better!"
- [SRC] "Impressive, outward turning horns" (S4, S5, S12). "Curl forward and terminate in sharp, outward turning tips" (S15). "Rappelant celles des mouflons", like a mouflon's (S8).
- [STD] Two accepted horn types, a result of the breed's rescue from several populations (S4):
  - the **Vendée / Jardin des Plantes** type: strong horns in an impressive, wide spiral away from the head;
  - the **Morbihan** type: smaller horns in a tighter spiral. Accepted in the UK, but scores lower at shows.
  - **Use the wide Vendée type** for the iconic look.

### 6.2 Exact path in side view (animal facing right, near horn = left horn)
Picture the coil as a clock face centred roughly on the base of the ear, slightly behind and below the eye [PHOTO: GEMO "excellent cornage" ram; OSS black ram in profile; OSS head drawing and points diagram]:

1. **Emergence, at about 12 to 1 o'clock.** The horn rises from the top of the skull (the poll), just behind and above the eye. The two horn bases sit close together on the crown, separated only by the woolly topknot. The base is the thickest part, about a quarter of the head length wide [EST].
2. **Up and back, from 12 to 10 o'clock.** It rises slightly, then sweeps backward (to the left in the image, toward the tail). The top of the coil is about level with the poll or slightly above it.
3. **Down behind the ear, at about 9 o'clock.** It curves down behind the ear, passing over the upper neck and the start of the mane.
4. **Under and forward, from 7 to 5 o'clock.** It wraps under the ear and comes forward. The lowest point of the coil sits at about the line of the lower jaw; on big-horned rams it hangs slightly below it.
5. **Tip, at about 4 to 3 o'clock.** The tip ends below and slightly behind the eye, at about the level of the mouth corner or jaw, **pointing forward and flaring outward** away from the cheek.

As seen by the viewer, the near horn therefore coils **anticlockwise** from base to tip. The spiral is not flat: it is a helix that moves outward, away from the skull, as it grows. That is why the tip stands clear of the cheek and turns outward, and why front views show the horns as a wide frame with the tips well outside the face (S4 photos).

### 6.3 How much it turns at maturity
- The standards say only "a single spiral of large amplitude". **No source gives an explicit number of turns.**
- [PHOTO] Growth stages:
  - **About 1 year:** roughly a half turn, sweeping out and down, tips at jaw level (GEMO's "Onyx, bélier de 1 an").
  - **Young adult:** about three quarters of a turn (OSS black ram in profile).
  - **Mature ram, 3–4 years and older:** about **one full turn (300–360°)**, as drawn in the OSS points diagram and head drawing and seen in the OSS white ram in profile.
- [EST] Old rams may go slightly beyond one turn, with the tip passing outside the base of the coil. "Single" spiral means one open curl, never a tight multi-turn corkscrew.

### 6.4 Size relative to the head
- [EST] In profile the coil's outer diameter is about **1.0–1.3 × the head length** (poll to nose), roughly 0.35–0.45 H tall.
- Seen from the side, the horn coil and the head together make the head silhouette almost round at the back, with the face projecting forward out of it.
- The eye, the bare face and the muzzle stay **fully clear of the horn**.

### 6.5 Distance from the face
- The required gap (a flat hand, ideally held horizontally) is **lateral**, so it cannot be measured in a pure profile.
- Suggest it in the drawing:
  - a cast shadow from the horn onto the cheek or neck, a little offset from the horn;
  - never letting the forward-running end of the horn overlap the cheek or eye as if touching it;
  - a turn of 5–10° toward the viewer, which shows the outward flare of the tip.
- **Do not draw a horn pressing into the cheek or growing toward the eye.** That is a fault and a welfare problem (S4, S1).

### 6.6 Cross-section and surface
- [STD] **Triangular in section, strong** (S1, S4). [PHOTO] From the side you mostly see the broad outer face. A sharper keel runs along the leading (top and front) edge of the curve.
- The horn tapers steadily from a massive base to a sharp tip (S15).
- [PHOTO] The surface carries dense **transverse ridges** (annulations):
  - a close series of fine, wavy ridges across the whole horn, deeper and more crowded near the base;
  - a few deeper grooves (the annual growth rings) spaced along the length;
  - paint them as alternating pale crests and dark grooves that wrap round the horn and follow the curve.
- The last few centimetres before the tip are smoother, slightly paler and worn.

### 6.7 Colour and layering
- **Colour:** see section 4.
  - Recommended: dark grey-brown, darker near the base, crests catching light, tip slightly paler.
  - Real black rams range from slate to khaki-beige (S4 note; [PHOTO]).
  - Both horns must match. Strong asymmetry disqualifies (S1).
- **Layering in pure profile:**
  - The near horn sits over the side of the head behind the eye, partly over the ear, and over the top of the neck and mane.
  - The far horn is almost entirely hidden. At most its upper arc shows as a thin second outline just behind or above the near horn's top. Its tip may peek out below the jaw or in front of the throat.

## 7. Legs and hooves
- [STD] "Fins, de longueur moyenne, bien proportionnés, bons aplombs": fine, of medium length, well proportioned, standing true (S1). OSS adds "good pasterns" (S4).
  - Faults: bow or cow hocks, sickle hocks, post legs, pigeon toes, knock knees, splayed feet (S4).
  - Disqualifications: overdeveloped limbs, or poor stance or hooves (S1).
- **Wool:** woolly down to the knee on the forelegs and the hock on the hind legs. Below that, slim black legs of short sleek hair (S1, S4, S9).
- **Bone:** fine and slender, but sound. Not spindly or deer-like like a Soay ("langen Beinen", long legs, S25), and not short and stubby (S2).
- **Correct profile line:**
  - Hind leg: a plumb line from the point of the buttock runs down the back of the hock and the back of the rear cannon (S4).
  - Foreleg: straight and vertical under the shoulder.
  - Pasterns: short and moderately sloped, neither upright nor low (S4).
- **Hooves:**
  - [STD] Dark for black sheep (S1, S4).
  - Small and neat, with two even toes held close together; a wide gap or crooked toes is a fault (S4).
  - "Very hard feet" (S13).
  - [EST] Each hoof is a small, slightly pointed black wedge, about as tall as the pastern is wide. The small dark dewclaws sit just above, at the back of the pastern.
- **Ram, in profile:** the scrotum shows between the hind legs below the belly fleece and is partly hidden by wool [PHOTO]. Draw it discreetly or leave it hidden by the fleece, depending on the use.

## 8. Temperament, behaviour, gait and voice (animation notes)

**Character**
- "Intelligent, inquisitive, gentle and full of character" (S4, S5, S12).
- "Friendlier than many other primitive breeds and easy to tame" (S5).
- "Attractive, lively, intelligent and friendly" (S14).
- "Very tame and easy to handle"; the rams are quiet and easy to manage (S13).
- "Bright and lively look" is written into the standard itself (S1).
- **Personality cue:** alert, curious and bold for its size. The Horniman's ewe is described as having "a BIG attitude", is "the first to start fights", and leads the flock's "baa-ing chorus" (S15).

**Flock instinct**
- Strongly gregarious. Never kept alone (it "will become distressed"); flocks of 3 to 10 (S4).
- Wikipedia FR reports neurasthenia with fewer than three animals (S6).
- The flock is hierarchical and submits to the dominant male (S3).
- When danger comes, the sheep bunch into a compact group and run away; they do not defend territory (S3).

**Hardiness and the island**
- Shaped by "the full force of Atlantic weather" and poor grazing on Ouessant (S4).
- Rustic: lives outdoors all year, with "bonne résistance au climat hivernal" (good resistance to winter weather) (S8).
- Grazed salt meadows by the sea (S4).
- Historical island life:
  - Thousands of sheep (about 6,000 around 1785) (S17).
  - Staked "au piquet pendant que les blés sont en terre" (on a stake while the grain was in the ground) (S17).
  - In the 1930s, "attachés deux par deux" (tethered in pairs) from April to September to keep them out of crops, and moved daily (S17). The GEMO site shows old images of "la tradition de l'attache et des déplacements quotidiens par les femmes" (S2).
  - Ownership marked by ear notches (S10).
  - Shelter from the wind behind low dry-stone or earth walls built as **three-branched stars**, so the flock could take the lee side whatever the wind direction (S18, S19).
  - These walls and a treeless heath on a granite cliff make a characteristic background.

**Grazing**
- "Le mouton passe beaucoup de temps à brouter en marchant" (spends much of its time grazing as it walks) (S3).
- Animation: head down, nibble, one or two slow steps, nibble again. The ears keep scanning, and the pupils stay horizontal (S20).
- Used as an "eco-mower": three sheep keep about 2,500 m² trimmed (S6, S11). Suits steep ground (S6).
- **Rumination:** lies down to chew the cud ("propension au repos couché", a tendency to lie down to rest) (S3).
  - Lying pose: on the brisket, legs folded under the body, head up, jaw grinding sideways.
  - Like other ruminants, it gets up **hind end first** (general ruminant behaviour).

**Senses and alertness**
- Good eyesight, sharp hearing and an excellent sense of smell (S3).
- Alert pose: head high, ears out sideways or rotated forward, body still, then a sudden turn or flight.

**Gait** (general sheep locomotion, plus the "lively" and "aptitude à la marche" notes in S3 and S14)
- Short, quick, light steps. A brisk, bouncy trot. Short bursts of canter when startled, often followed by a stop and an about-turn to stare.
- Small springy jumps onto rocks or banks.
- Lambs are "quick to their feet" (S5). They frisk and leap with all four legs off the ground.
- Rams spar by backing up and butting heads. The horns stay rigid with the skull.

**Voice**
- No breed-specific call is documented. Use the standard sheep repertoire (S21):
  - **high bleat**, mouth wide open, louder and higher, given when separated from the flock or stressed: the classic "baa";
  - **low bleat**, mouth closed, a soft murmur given in close contact (ewe and lamb).
- Each sheep has an individual vocal signature (S21).
- Animation of a high bleat: head raised, neck stretched forward, lower jaw dropped, upper lip slightly lifted.
- A lone animal calling for its flock fits the documented distress at isolation (S4).

**Lifespan:** 13 to 18 years (S12).

## 9. The 10 details that say "Ouessant", not Soay or Hebridean

1. **Tiny but correctly proportioned.**
   - Rams up to 49 cm and ewes up to 46 cm. A level-backed rectangular body with fine legs of medium length (S1, S2, S4).
   - Not dwarfed and not leggy. The Soay is taller (OSS: 51–61 cm rams; LfL: ewes 45–50 cm) and "slim with long legs" (S4, S25).
   - The Hebridean is much heavier: ewes 35–40 kg (S22).
2. **One pair of strong horns, rams only, each a single wide spiral round the ear**, standing clear of the face with the tips flaring outward (S1, S4, S6, S15).
   - Hebrideans often carry four or more horns, and both sexes are horned (S22, S23).
   - Soay rams have big round downward horns "ohne Spiralbildung", without a spiral (S25).
3. **Ewes hornless.** In Hebridean and Soay ewes, horns or scurs are common (S22, S24, S25).
4. **One solid, uniform colour: here, black.**
   - No mouflon pattern: no white belly, no pale rump patch, no light chin or eye rings as in the Soay (S24, S25).
   - No white markings; piebald disqualifies (S1).
5. **A woolly topknot on the forehead and partly over the cheeks, with a bare black face below the eyes** (S1, S6). The Hebridean has no wool on the face or legs (S23). The Soay has a short-haired face.
6. **A true long-wool fleece**: semi-closed wavy locks of 8–10 cm reaching the knees and hocks, shorn every year (S1, S4, S10). The Soay has a short 3–5 cm coat that it sheds naturally (S24, S25).
7. **The ram's "cravate"**: a long, coarse, dark mane on the throat and nape (S1, S4).
8. **A short, thin tail ending above the hocks, natural and never fluke-shaped.** The Hebridean has a "short, triangular tail" (S4, S23).
9. **Jet-black face and legs against a fleece that sun-browns to rusty tips.** This contrast is described in the standards (S1, S4, S9) and visible in every reference photo.
10. **A fine, straight-profiled head with small, rounded, horizontally carried ears and pale golden eyes in a black face.** The ram has only a very slight Roman nose; no animal is dish-faced (S1, S4). Hebridean ewes are "slightly dished" (S22).

**Common mistakes to avoid:**
- wattles or a dewlap;
- any white spot;
- a long or triangular tail;
- big or drooping ears;
- a Roman-nosed ewe or a dish-faced animal;
- horns touching the face, horns that are too straight, or four horns;
- a horned ewe;
- wool socks below the knees or hocks;
- a smooth Merino-like fleece;
- stubby "dwarf" legs, or long Soay-like legs;
- a lamb-sized head on an adult.

### 10.3 Where sources disagree, and reliability notes
- **Weight:** French sources give 11–16 kg (ewe) and 12/13–20 kg (ram). ASSONAPA gives 13–15 and 15–20 kg. Rivista di Agraria gives 15–23 kg. Farmers Weekly gives about 11 kg and CNCH 16–23 kg. Neither the GEMO nor the OSS standard sets a weight.
- **Fibre diameter:** GEMO says 27–28 µm, OSS 25–28 µm, ASSONAPA 25–55 µm (probably including the coarse hair and kemp).
- **Fleece weight:** GEMO says 4–4.5 % of live weight, which works out to about 0.5–0.9 kg. Rivista and ASSONAPA give 1.0–1.5 kg. CNCH says "about 10 % of body weight".
- **Horn colour:** the standard asks for dark horns on black sheep. The OSS admits they "may not be as dark as the fleece". Photos range from slate to pale khaki.
- **Horn tightness:** the UK accepts the tighter Morbihan type, with a lower show score. GEMO disqualifies horns that are "trop serrées" or "trop écartées" (too tight or too widely splayed).
- **Number of horn turns:** not stated by any source. The "about one full turn" in 6.3 comes from photos and the OSS drawings.
- **Ear carriage:** GEMO says "tendant à se dresser" (tending to prick), Wikipedia FR "dressées" (erect), ASSONAPA "tendenti a stare erette". The OSS clarifies that this means carried near horizontal and pointing outward, not vertical. Photos agree with the OSS.
- **Wattles:** a disqualification in France (S1), excluded from breeding in Italy (S9), "permitted but not desirable" in the UK (S4).
- **Soay size:** the OSS gives 51–61 cm for rams and 49–54 cm for ewes. LfL Bayern gives ewes 45–50 cm, and its ram row is ambiguous (probably 50–55 cm).
- **History dates:** the OSS guide says the breed was reconstructed "in the 1960s". French sources date the rescue to 1976, the year GEMO was founded. The GEMO booklet says the standard was approved in March 1981, while the standard page says it was proposed in December 1981 and amended in 1991.
- **GEMO's own website** could not be located or reached. The standard text was taken from the "Les amis du mouton d'Ouessant" site, which reproduces it with the GEMO assembly dates. It is cross-checked against the OSS's English rendering (S4), the ASSONAPA sheet (S9) and Wikipedia FR (S6), which cites the same page.
- **Wikimedia Commons** has about 45 Ouessant photos, but they could not be downloaded because of rate limiting.
- **Body length, head length, ear and horn ratios and the leg-to-depth ratio** are estimates measured off the reference photos and drawings. No published figures were found.
